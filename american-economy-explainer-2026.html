Chapter 01
The Gold Standard & 1971
How money worked for most of human history, what Bretton Woods was, why Nixon ended it on a Sunday night in August 1971, and what changed forever.

What Is the Gold Standard?

A gold standard means the government's currency is directly redeemable for a fixed quantity of gold. If you hold a $20 bill, the government promises you can exchange it for $20 worth of physical gold. This means the government can only issue as much currency as it has gold to back it — it cannot simply print more money out of thin air.

The US was on some version of a gold standard from 1792 until 1971 — nearly 180 years. Gold acted as a hard constraint on government spending and money creation. It also meant long-run prices were remarkably stable: a dollar in 1800 bought roughly what a dollar bought in 1900.

Why Gold?

Gold has been money across virtually every civilization in history because of its properties: it cannot be created artificially, it does not corrode, it is divisible, portable, and universally recognized. Its scarcity is guaranteed by physics, not by government promises.

Bretton Woods (1944–1971)

In July 1944, with World War II still ongoing, 44 Allied nations gathered at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire to design the post-war monetary order. The architects were John Maynard Keynes (UK) and Harry Dexter White (US).

Everyone remembered how the chaotic currency devaluations of the 1930s had deepened the Great Depression and fueled political instability. They wanted a stable, rules-based system.

How Bretton Woods Worked

  • The US dollar was pegged to gold at $35 per ounce — any foreign government could redeem dollars for gold at that fixed price, on demand
  • Every other currency was pegged to the dollar at a fixed exchange rate
  • The dollar became the world's reserve currency — the thing all international trade was settled in
  • Two new institutions were created: the IMF (to manage balance of payment crises) and the World Bank (to fund reconstruction)

This gave the US an enormous privilege: because everyone needed dollars to trade, the US could run deficits that other nations couldn't. French President de Gaulle famously called this the "exorbitant privilege."

Why It Eventually Broke Down — The Triffin Dilemma

Yale economist Robert Triffin identified a fatal structural contradiction in the early 1960s: to supply the world with dollars, the US had to run trade deficits. But running deficits meant printing more dollars than gold could back — which undermined confidence in the $35/oz peg. You couldn't do both indefinitely. It was built to fail.

The Vietnam War and LBJ's domestic spending programs made it worse — more dollars flooded the world with less gold backing each one. De Gaulle started aggressively redeeming French dollar reserves for physical gold. Other nations followed. US gold reserves fell from ~20,000 tons after WWII to ~8,000 tons by 1971. A slow-motion bank run was underway.

The Cantillon Effect — Who Benefits From New Money

Named for 18th-century economist Richard Cantillon: newly created money doesn't spread evenly. It flows first through financial markets and credit — inflating asset prices (homes, stocks) before wages catch up. People who own assets get richer. People who only sell their labor fall behind. This effect has run continuously since 1971.

The Federal Reserve
What it is, how it was created in 1913, what it actually does, and how it connects to inflation, interest rates, and everything else on this page.

What Is the Federal Reserve?

The Federal Reserve ("the Fed") is the central bank of the United States. It is a quasi-governmental institution — created by Congress in 1913, but structured to operate independently from direct political control. It is not a private bank but it is not a typical government agency either; it exists in a deliberate gray zone.

Why Was It Created?

The US had no central bank for much of the 19th century and suffered repeated banking panics — 1873, 1893, 1907. In 1907, J.P. Morgan personally had to organize a private bailout to prevent the financial system from collapsing. It was obvious that leaving crisis management to one private banker was not sustainable.

Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act in December 1913, creating a central bank to act as a "lender of last resort" — an institution that could inject liquidity into the system during panics to prevent bank runs from cascading into full collapses.

Jekyll Island — The Secret Meeting

In November 1910, a group of the most powerful bankers in America secretly gathered at Jekyll Island, Georgia, disguised as a duck hunting trip. They drafted what became the Federal Reserve Act. The group included representatives of Rockefeller, Morgan, and Rothschild banking interests. This meeting is documented historical fact — not conspiracy theory — and is openly discussed in the Fed's own historical accounts.

What the Fed Actually Does

1. Sets Interest Rates (The Federal Funds Rate)

The Fed's most powerful tool is setting the federal funds rate — the interest rate at which banks lend money to each other overnight. This rate cascades through the entire economy: it affects mortgage rates, car loan rates, credit card rates, business borrowing costs, and by extension, economic activity and inflation.

  • Low rates → cheap borrowing → more spending and investment → economic stimulus → can cause inflation
  • High rates → expensive borrowing → less spending → economic slowdown → reduces inflation

2. Controls the Money Supply

The Fed can expand or contract the amount of money in the financial system through open market operations — buying or selling US Treasury bonds. When the Fed buys bonds, it creates new money and injects it into the banking system. When it sells bonds, it removes money.

Post-2008, the Fed dramatically expanded this with Quantitative Easing (QE) — buying trillions of dollars of bonds and mortgage-backed securities, expanding its balance sheet from ~$900 billion in 2008 to over $9 trillion by 2022.

3. Regulates Banks

The Fed supervises and regulates large commercial banks, sets reserve requirements, and acts as lender of last resort — providing emergency lending to banks that are solvent but facing liquidity crises.

4. The Dual Mandate

Unlike most central banks, the Fed has a dual mandate from Congress: maintain price stability (low inflation) AND maximum employment. These goals sometimes conflict — lowering unemployment can require stimulating the economy in ways that cause inflation.

How the Fed Creates Money — "Out of Thin Air"

This is the part that surprises most people. When the Fed buys a Treasury bond from a bank, it doesn't transfer existing money — it literally creates new dollars as a digital entry. The money did not exist before the transaction. This is not controversial — it is how modern central banking works and the Fed explains it this way in its own educational materials.

Fractional Reserve Banking Multiplies This Further

Banks are only required to hold a fraction of their deposits in reserve. When the Fed creates $1 million and deposits it in the banking system, banks can lend out most of it. That lending creates deposits at other banks, which lend out most of those deposits, and so on. The original $1 million can become $8–10 million in total money supply through this multiplication process. The Fed effectively controls the starting point; the banking system multiplies it.

The Fed and Inflation — The 2021-2023 Case Study

In 2020-2021, the Fed and Treasury injected approximately $5 trillion into the economy through stimulus checks, expanded unemployment benefits, and QE. This was the largest peacetime monetary expansion in US history. By 2021-2022, inflation hit 9.1% — the highest since 1981.

The Fed then raised interest rates from near zero to over 5% in the fastest rate-hiking cycle since the 1980s to bring inflation back down. By 2024 inflation was ~3%, but prices remained at their elevated levels — inflation falling does not mean prices go back down, it just means they stop rising as fast.

The Austrian Critique of the Fed

Austrian economists argue the Fed is the root cause of the boom-bust cycle. Artificially low interest rates encourage excessive borrowing and malinvestment — businesses and individuals take on more risk than they would with market-set rates. Eventually the excess is revealed, causing a crash. The Fed then responds with more stimulus, creating the next bubble. They call this "Cantillon enrichment" — each cycle transfers wealth from savers and wage earners to asset holders who benefit from cheap money first.

What This Actually Means

From 1948 to 1971, productivity and wages moved in near-lockstep — when workers produced more, they earned more. After 1971, the lines decoupled. Workers kept producing more every year, but that value flowed disproportionately to capital (shareholders, executives, asset owners) rather than to the workers creating it. This is the central economic story of the last 50 years.

The Major Causes

1. Globalization and Labor Arbitrage

From roughly 1980–2010, manufacturing jobs were offshored to China, Mexico, Vietnam, and other low-wage countries at massive scale. This created a global labor competition — US factory workers were now implicitly competing with workers earning $2–5/day. Wages for non-college workers in manufacturing-heavy regions were suppressed directly.

China's entry into the WTO in 2001 alone is estimated to have eliminated 2–2.4 million US manufacturing jobs. The communities affected — the Rust Belt, parts of the South — never fully recovered. This is often called the "China Shock" and is well-documented in academic economics.

2. Union Decline

Unions were historically the mechanism by which productivity gains were shared with workers. A union negotiates collective contracts that extract a portion of company profits as wages. When union power collapsed, that extraction mechanism disappeared.

  • 1935 — Wagner Act protects collective bargaining rights; unions grow rapidly
  • 1947 — Taft-Hartley Act restricts union tactics; growth slows
  • 1981 — Reagan fires 11,000 striking air traffic controllers (PATCO); signals government will not protect unions against employer retaliation
  • 1980s–2000s — Corporate restructuring, offshoring, and hostile labor law enforcement accelerate membership collapse
  • 2018 — Janus v. AFSCME Supreme Court ruling weakens public sector unions further

3. Automation and Technology

Machines replaced routine, repetitive labor — assembly line work, data entry, routine cognitive tasks. This increased productivity (machines do more per hour) but reduced demand for those workers and suppressed their wages. Technology displaced middle-skill, middle-wage jobs disproportionately — a phenomenon economists call "hollowing out" of the labor market.

4. Fiat Money and the Cantillon Effect

Post-1971 monetary expansion inflated asset prices faster than wages. Someone who owned a home in 1971 saw their net worth increase with each round of monetary stimulus. A worker who only had wages saw their purchasing power erode. Over 50 years, this systematically transferred wealth from wage-earners to asset-holders.

5. Corporate Power and Monopsony

In many labor markets, especially smaller cities and specific industries, there is only one or a few major employers. Economists call this monopsony — the buyer of labor has market power. Workers can't simply leave for a competitor. This gives employers downward wage-setting power. Hospital systems, Amazon distribution centers, and agricultural employers in rural areas are common examples. Non-compete agreements (affecting ~30 million workers as of 2020) also suppress wages by limiting workers' ability to move to higher-paying jobs.

6. Shareholder Primacy and the Friedman Doctrine

In 1970, economist Milton Friedman published an influential essay arguing corporations' only responsibility is to maximize shareholder returns. Through the 1980s–90s, this philosophy became dominant in American boardrooms. Companies began systematically cutting labor costs, eliminating benefits, moving to part-time and contract work, and distributing profits as dividends and buybacks rather than wages. Executive pay, tied to stock performance, exploded — from ~20x worker pay in 1965 to ~350x by 2020.

The K-Shaped Recovery Pattern

Since at least 2008, economic recoveries have been "K-shaped" — those in the top half of the income distribution see incomes, wealth, and asset values recover and grow. Those in the bottom half see stagnant wages and deteriorating living standards. The Fed's post-crisis stimulus primarily inflates asset prices, which only helps people who own assets. The K-shape has gotten more pronounced with every crisis cycle.

Why Housing Got So Expensive

1. Zoning and Supply Restrictions — The Primary Driver

This is the most important and most underdiscussed cause. American cities have made it extraordinarily difficult to build new housing through:

  • Single-family zoning — large portions of most US cities are legally prohibited from having apartments or multi-family buildings
  • Minimum lot size requirements — you can't subdivide lots to build more units
  • Parking minimums — every unit must include expensive parking even near transit
  • Height limits — prevent density even where land values justify it
  • Environmental review requirements (CEQA in California) — can delay projects for years
  • NIMBYism (Not In My Backyard) — existing homeowners attend zoning hearings and block new supply because scarcity protects their home values

The proof: Tokyo, one of the world's largest cities, has loose zoning and permissive building rules — and housing is remarkably affordable relative to income, despite similar monetary policy. Supply restrictions explain most of the US problem independently of monetary policy.

2. Monetary Policy and Asset Price Inflation

Post-1971 monetary expansion and post-2008 near-zero interest rates caused homes to be treated as investment assets rather than places to live. When you can borrow at 3%, home prices rise until the monthly payment consumes a "normal" share of income — which means much higher prices at low rates.

Investor and institutional purchasing of single-family homes (hedge funds, REITs) further competed for limited supply, treating housing as a financial instrument rather than shelter.

3. Construction Cost Increases

  • Labor costs — construction workers have captured some productivity gains
  • Building codes — expanded requirements for energy efficiency, accessibility, and safety are good but add cost
  • Materials inflation — lumber, copper, concrete costs increased significantly especially post-2020
  • Permitting fees — some cities charge $50,000–$100,000+ in fees per unit before construction begins

4. The 2022–2024 Rate Lock-In Effect

When the Fed raised rates from 3% to 7%+, homeowners who had locked in 3% mortgages were unwilling to sell — selling meant giving up a 3% rate and buying a new home at 7%, nearly doubling the monthly payment. This froze supply further and kept prices elevated even as demand fell. A perverse consequence of the rate cycle.

Why Cars Got So Expensive

The average new car price in 2024 is approximately $48,000, up from ~$25,000 in 2000 (inflation-adjusted). Monthly payments on new cars now average over $700, consuming a larger share of income than ever. Here is why:

1. Feature Creep and Regulation

Modern cars include mandated safety equipment (airbags, backup cameras, collision warning systems), emissions controls, and fuel economy technology that add thousands of dollars per vehicle. The average car has gone from containing ~$2,000 in electronics in 2000 to ~$15,000+ today. Consumers also expect features — touchscreens, heated seats, advanced driver assistance — that have become standard. All of this is in the base price.

2. Supply Chain Disruptions (2020–2022)

COVID-19 caused a global semiconductor shortage. Cars contain hundreds of chips — for entertainment systems, safety features, engine management. Without chips, manufacturers couldn't build cars. Inventory collapsed, dealers charged above MSRP for the first time in decades, and prices exploded. The habit of paying over sticker price stuck even as supply recovered.

3. Consolidation and Reduced Competition

The US auto market is increasingly dominated by large trucks and SUVs where margins are highest. Manufacturers deliberately exited the affordable small-car segment — Ford stopped selling most cars in the US by 2019. The cheapest segments are now left to a few import brands. Less competition in the affordable range means higher floor prices.

4. Financialization of Auto Buying

Dealers and manufacturers shifted focus from purchase price to monthly payment. Longer loan terms (72 and 84-month loans are now common vs. 36-48 months previously) let buyers afford higher prices at the same monthly payment. But you pay far more in total interest, and many buyers end up "underwater" — owing more than the car is worth. This psychological pricing trick helped inflate prices over decades.

The Real Affordability Picture

In 1970, a median-income worker could buy a median home with roughly 4 years of gross salary. They could buy a new car with about 4–5 months of salary. Today those same ratios are ~10 years for a home and ~8–9 months for a car. The cost of the two largest purchases in most Americans' lives has roughly doubled relative to income in 50 years.

The Root Problem: Nobody Pays Directly

In almost every functional market, buyers feel prices and respond to them. Healthcare systematically broke this mechanism. When a third party — an insurer or government program — pays the bill, patients don't shop on price and providers have no incentive to compete on price. This is called the third-party payer problem and it is the foundation of all other healthcare cost issues.

How It Happened — Layer by Layer

Layer 1 — WWII Wage Controls (1942)

During WWII, the government froze wages to prevent inflation. Employers couldn't attract workers by raising salaries, so they offered health insurance as a non-wage benefit. The IRS ruled employer-provided health insurance was tax-exempt. This was a wartime accident that became the permanent foundation of the US healthcare system.

The tax exemption is worth ~$300 billion annually — the largest single tax expenditure in the US federal budget. It locks in employer-tied insurance and ensures most Americans never see the true cost of their care.

Layer 2 — Medicare and Medicaid (1965)

LBJ's Great Society created Medicare (elderly) and Medicaid (low-income), covering roughly 40% of Americans. When the government becomes a massive payer with essentially unlimited ability to pay, two things happen: providers raise prices because the payer can't easily say no, and demand increases because insured patients use more care than they would paying out of pocket.

Layer 3 — The Insurance Design Problem

Insurance is supposed to cover catastrophic, unpredictable events — not routine, predictable ones. Car insurance doesn't pay for oil changes. But US health insurance pays for everything, including routine office visits, annual checkups, and standard prescriptions. Every small claim requires an administrative process — coding, submission, review, negotiation — that costs money at every step. Estimates suggest 25–35 cents of every healthcare dollar goes to administration rather than care.

Layer 4 — Hospital Consolidation

The US has seen massive hospital consolidation since the 1990s. Large health systems have bought up competitors, independent hospitals, and physician practices. In many metropolitan areas, one or two systems control most of the market. This market power allows them to charge insurers whatever the traffic will bear — and insurers pass those costs to employers and workers through higher premiums. Studies consistently show that hospital mergers increase prices 20–40% with no improvement in quality.

Layer 5 — Drug Pricing and the Free Rider Problem

The US funds most of the world's pharmaceutical research through high drug prices. Other countries use government bargaining power to pay low prices — a new drug that costs $10,000/year in the US costs $1,000–2,000 in Germany or Canada. US patients subsidize global access. This is partly why the US pays 2–5x more for the same drugs than peer nations.

Additionally, the FDA approval process is expensive, patent protection grants long monopoly periods (often extended by minor formulation changes), and pharmacy benefit managers extract significant rents as middlemen.

Layer 6 — Certificate of Need Laws

Many states require hospitals and clinics to get government permission before adding capacity — new beds, MRI machines, surgical centers. These "Certificate of Need" laws were meant to prevent overbuilding but became tools for existing hospitals to block competition. Less competition equals higher prices.

Why It Doesn't Self-Correct

Healthcare doesn't work like a normal market: demand is inelastic (you can't shop during an emergency), supply is restricted (medical licensing, regulations), the payer and consumer are different parties, and the most powerful actors in the system profit from its complexity. Hospital systems, insurers, pharma companies, and PBMs (pharmacy benefit managers) collectively spend hundreds of millions on lobbying annually to preserve the status quo.

The Bennett Hypothesis — The Core Mechanism

In 1987, US Secretary of Education William Bennett argued: "Increases in financial aid in recent years have enabled colleges and universities blithely to raise their tuitions." This is known as the Bennett Hypothesis and decades of evidence has largely confirmed it.

The Textbook Mechanism

When the government makes it easy for anyone to borrow money for college — regardless of the degree's market value or the school's quality — it removes price sensitivity from the buyer. Students don't feel the cost of a $60,000/year tuition when it's financed by a loan they won't repay for 20 years. Colleges, knowing students can always borrow more, raise prices to capture the available subsidy. This is identical to what happened in healthcare and housing — government financing detaches the buyer from the price.

Congress periodically increases the amount students can borrow. Each increase is followed by tuition increases that consume the new headroom. Colleges learn that any available loan money will be absorbed by price increases.

The Student Aid and Fiscal Responsibility Act moves all federal student lending from private banks to the Department of Education. The government is now the direct lender to virtually all students. Credit standards are essentially non-existent — any enrolled student qualifies regardless of major, school quality, or employment prospects.
2010s–2020s
Income-Driven Repayment and Forgiveness
As debt loads become unmanageable, the government creates income-driven repayment plans and forgiveness programs. These further reduce the borrower's perceived cost — "I'll only pay a small percentage of my income for 20 years" — removing the last vestige of price discipline.

Other Contributing Factors

The Administrative Bloat Explosion

Between 1993 and 2009, the number of university administrators grew by 60% while the number of faculty grew 6%. Universities have built enormous administrative bureaucracies — diversity offices, student affairs departments, marketing teams, compliance offices, title IX coordinators. This administrative overhead is a major driver of cost increases that has nothing to do with instruction quality.

The Arms Race in Amenities

Universities compete for students by building amenities — climbing walls, luxury dorms, gourmet dining, state-of-the-art recreation centers. Because students pay with loans rather than their own money, they respond to quality-of-life marketing rather than price. Universities that skimped on amenities lost enrollment to those that splurged. The result was an amenity arms race financed entirely by student debt.

State Disinvestment in Public Universities

State governments gradually shifted funding away from public universities from the 1970s onward. A public university that once received 70% of its funding from the state now might receive 20–30%. Schools responded by raising tuition — which was politically easier than fighting for state appropriations. The federal loan system made those tuition increases absorbable, reinforcing the cycle.

Credential Inflation

As a bachelor's degree became the baseline credential for middle-class employment, demand became inelastic — you "had to" go to college to access certain job markets. This reduced students' price sensitivity further; declining to attend because of cost meant accepting a lower-tier career path. College sold not just education but access to credentialed labor markets.

The Irony of Well-Intentioned Policy

Every expansion of student loan programs was intended to make college more affordable and accessible. The result was that college became less affordable while access expanded. This is a near-perfect case study in how government subsidies, absent market discipline, are captured by suppliers (universities) as price increases rather than passed through to consumers as savings. The same dynamic explains healthcare cost growth.

How Did We Get Here?

The US national debt in 1971, the year Nixon ended the gold standard, was approximately $398 billion. Today it exceeds $34 trillion — an 85-fold increase in nominal terms. The connection is not coincidental. Under a gold standard, the government could not print money to cover deficits; it had to borrow at market rates with real constraints on how much the market would lend. Under fiat money, the Fed can buy government bonds — effectively printing money to finance deficits.

Does the Debt Matter? The Competing Views

The "It Matters Enormously" View (Mainstream / Austrian)

  • Interest burden is crowding out other spending — $1T+/year in interest is money that can't go to defense, infrastructure, or tax cuts. Interest is now the single largest federal expenditure category.
  • It is inflationary — financing deficits with money creation (via the Fed buying Treasuries) expands the money supply and contributes to inflation
  • Future generations inherit the burden — today's spending is tomorrow's taxes or inflation
  • Risk of a debt spiral — if interest rates stay elevated and debt keeps growing, interest payments consume an ever-larger share of tax revenue, requiring more borrowing, creating a self-reinforcing cycle
  • Reserve currency risk — if confidence in the dollar as reserve currency erodes, the US loses its "exorbitant privilege" to borrow cheaply, and rates spike

The MMT View (Modern Monetary Theory)

MMT economists (Stephanie Kelton, et al.) argue the conventional framing is wrong for a country that issues its own currency:

  • The US cannot "run out" of dollars — it creates them. The debt will never be "paid off" in the traditional sense and doesn't need to be.
  • The real constraint on government spending is not debt but inflation — you can spend until you create too much inflation, then pull back
  • Federal "debt" is really just dollars the government has injected into the private sector and not yet taxed back — it is private sector savings
  • The 2020 experiment (massive stimulus with initially mild inflation) is cited as supporting evidence
The Practical Problem With MMT Right Now

Whatever the theoretical validity, the US is currently paying over $1 trillion per year in interest — more than the entire defense budget. This is not hypothetical future risk; it is a present fiscal reality that is crowding out other priorities and absorbing an increasing share of every tax dollar collected. At current trajectory, interest alone will consume 25%+ of federal revenue by 2030.

The Reserve Currency Wildcard

The US can carry debt that would sink other nations because the dollar is the world's reserve currency — everyone needs dollars to buy oil, trade globally, and hold reserves. This gives the US uniquely low borrowing costs. If the dollar ever loses reserve currency status — a slow process that could take decades — the US would face dramatically higher interest rates on its debt, triggering a fiscal crisis. China's attempts to settle trade in yuan and the growth of alternative payment systems are very early signals worth watching over the long term.
